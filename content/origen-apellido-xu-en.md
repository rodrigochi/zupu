This *zupu*, like almost all traditional Chinese genealogies, traces the lineage back to an ancestor of the mythical era: **Boyi** (伯益), a figure from roughly 4,000 years ago. Before telling that story, it is worth explaining how Chinese surnames work — because they work very differently from Western ones.

---

## Chinese surnames don't work like ours

In the West, surnames are relatively recent. Most appeared in the Middle Ages, about 800 years ago, and typically describe a trade (*Smith*, *Herrero*), a place (*Hill*, *Del Río*) or a father's name (*Johnson*, *Rodríguez*, "son of Rodrigo").

The oldest Chinese surnames are more than 4,000 years old, and nearly all derive from the nobility: they were the name of a kingdom, a territory granted by a sovereign, or an ancestor whose merit was recorded. For much of Chinese history, ordinary people had no surname — having one was a privilege of recognised lineages.

This produces two practical differences. **The surname comes first**: 徐先生 is "Xu Mister", not "Mister Xu". And **women do not change their surname upon marriage**: in this *zupu*, wives always appear under their clan of origin — 67 different clans across 30 generations.

The surname **徐** — Mandarin *Xú*, Cantonese *Tsui*, Hakka *Chí* (hence "Chi" in Chile) — is the eleventh most common in China, with around 30 million bearers.

## What our *zupu* says

<div class="art-zupu">
<div class="art-zupu-zh">徐氏之譜系出伯益</div>
<div class="art-zupu-tr">"The lineage of the Xu descends from Boyi" — <em>Zupu</em> of Changxing Li</div>
</div>

The tradition recorded by the clan's genealogists reaches even further back: the Xu of Donghai are said to descend from Zhuanxu (顓頊), one of the Five Mythical Emperors, grandson of the Yellow Emperor.

This requires context, because it may sound like fabrication. Virtually all the great Chinese clans do the same: the Li claim descent from Laozi, the Kong from Confucius, the Liu from the Han emperors. Connecting the lineage to a figure of the legendary era is a convention of the genre — comparable to European noble houses that claimed descent from Charlemagne or the Trojans. The mythical ancestor is not a verifiable biological fact; it is the traditional way of declaring the antiquity and prestige of the clan.

The *zupu* itself treats it as such. After naming Boyi, it notes that part of the record is too ancient to reconstruct with clarity, and that the verifiable genealogy begins with Nianjiulang (廿九郎) — Xu Heling (徐鶴齡) of Shicheng — at the end of the Song dynasty, about 900 years ago. This explicit distinction between inherited tradition and verifiable records is typical of well-compiled *zupu*.

So: who was Boyi?

---

## The myth: a hero, a flood, a kingdom

<figure class="art-figure-right">
<img src="./assets/boyi.png" alt="Boyi (伯益), helper of Yu the Great">
<figcaption>Boyi (伯益)<br>Helper of Yu the Great<br>~2000 BCE</figcaption>
</figure>

The story takes place in China's legendary era, where myth and history interweave — the equivalent of Romulus and Remus for the Romans.

According to tradition, China was then suffering the **Great Flood**, an inundation that lasted two generations. The one who managed to control it was **Yu the Great** (大禹), one of the foundational figures of Chinese civilisation. His achievement lay in changing strategy: instead of building dikes to hold the water back — a method that had failed for decades — he dug channels to direct it to the sea. Legend says that in thirteen years of work he passed his own house three times and did not go in.

**Boyi** (伯益) was his chief assistant: leader of an eastern tribe whose totem was the bird. As a reward, his family received a territory along the Huai River, where his son Ruomu (若木) founded a state called **Xu** (徐). His descendants took the name of the state as their family name.

## The State of Xu

The State of Xu is not pure legend: it appears in the **oracle bones** of the Shang dynasty and in **bronze inscriptions** of the Zhou dynasty. According to tradition, it existed for nearly 1,600 years — longer than the Roman Empire.

<figure class="art-figure">
<img src="./assets/xu_state.svg" alt="The State of Xu and neighbouring kingdoms">
<figcaption>The State of Xu and neighbouring kingdoms · Warring States period (~500 BCE)</figcaption>
</figure>

Its most remembered king is **Xu Yanwang** (徐偃王), celebrated for his benevolent rule: dozens of neighbouring states acknowledged his authority without having been conquered. When the Zhou central power marched against him, Yanwang had the means to fight and chose not to: he withdrew to the mountains to avoid war, and according to tradition thousands of people followed him. His name, *Yan* (偃), means "to lay down arms". He is the moral reference figure of the surname.

On the end of the kingdom, traditions differ — normal for accounts of this antiquity. The most cited version says the State of Wu destroyed it in **512 BCE** by diverting a river to flood the capital — with **Sun Tzu**, the author of *The Art of War*, among its generals. What all versions agree on is the outcome: the descendants of the destroyed kingdom kept its name as a surname.

## Donghai and Nanzhou

In the opening poetry of our *zupu* appears this verse:

<div class="art-verse">
<div class="art-verse-zh">南洲系族發三江<br>東海源流出太洋</div>
<div class="art-verse-tr">"The lineage of Nanzhou flourished by the Three Rivers;<br>the current of Donghai flows from the great ocean."</div>
</div>

**Donghai** (東海) and **Nanzhou** (南洲) are the two classic identity markers of the Xu clan, appearing in temples and genealogies throughout China and its diaspora.

**Donghai** is the clan's *junwang*: the "place of lineage prestige". It is a concept with no direct Western equivalent — it does not indicate where a family lives, but rather from which historical region its lineage originates. Donghai, in southern Shandong, corresponds to the territory of the ancient State of Xu. Hence the saying *天下徐氏出東海* — "all the Xu of the world come from Donghai" — and the clan's most common hall name: Donghai Tang (東海堂).

**Nanzhou** refers to a specific person: **Xu Ruzi** (徐孺子), a scholar of the Eastern Han dynasty, remembered for his learning and filial piety. The governor of his region kept a bed reserved exclusively for his visits — an anecdote immortalised in the *Pavilion of Prince Teng*, one of the most celebrated texts in Chinese literature. His name passed into history as *南州高士*, "the great scholar of Nanzhou".

Hence many Xu families use this pair of inscriptions:

<div class="art-inscriptions">
<div class="art-inscription">
<div class="art-inscription-zh">東海源流遠</div>
<div class="art-inscription-tr">"The source of Donghai<br>reaches far back"</div>
</div>
<div class="art-inscription">
<div class="art-inscription-zh">南洲世澤長</div>
<div class="art-inscription-tr">"The legacy of Nanzhou<br>endures through generations"</div>
</div>
</div>

One points to the kingdom of origin; the other, to the model of virtue. That this same pair appears in the *zupu* of a Hakka family from Guangdong whose descendants today live in Chile shows how far this tradition has reached.

## From Donghai to Niánkēng, from Niánkēng to Chile

After the fall of the kingdom, the Xu took part in the great migrations of Chinese history. One of those currents was that of the **Hakka** (客家) — "guest families" — northern groups who migrated over centuries toward the mountains of Guangdong, preserving their language and a strong genealogical tradition.

This *zupu* belongs to that tradition: the 徐 clan of Niánkēng (粘坑), Guangdong, with about 900 years of verifiable records, from Nianjiulang at the end of the Song dynasty to the Chi family in Chile.

It is worth closing by explaining what a *zupu* (族譜) actually is, because it differs from Western genealogy. In the West, genealogy tends to be a personal investigation backwards: someone decides to reconstruct their family tree and searches for records. The *zupu* works in the opposite direction: it is a collective record of the clan, maintained **forwards**, generation after generation, for centuries. It was updated periodically, reprinted, and someone in the clan was responsible for preserving it through wars and migrations. **This site is the continuation of that record.**
